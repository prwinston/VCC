Why you need Jesus

The question
underneath
every question.

You have felt it late at night — the sense that there should be more. This is an honest, unhurried answer. No guilt. No pressure. Just the truth, and a family ready to walk it with you.

A 9-minute read·Written for the sceptic, the seeker & the tired

It is 1:47 a.m. The blue light of your phone has burned itself into your eyes. You have scrolled past forty lives that look better than yours, answered three work messages you resent, and muted a group chat that somehow makes you lonelier. The room is quiet except for the hum of the aircon and the sound of your own heartbeat, a little too fast. You put the phone face-down. And in the dark, a small, unwelcome thought surfaces: Is this it?

You are not broken for asking. You are awake. That ache — the one that no promotion, no relationship, no holiday to Bali ever fully silenced — is not a design flaw. It is a signpost. Every human culture in every century has felt it and named it differently: the Greeks called it a restless longing, the poets call it the ache for home, your grandmother called it a soul. You were built for more than you have been given, and you know it. The only real question is what you do with that knowing.

Most of us do one of two things. We numb it — more content, more wine, more work, more noise — or we chase it, sprinting after the next achievement that promises to finally make us feel whole. Both are exhausting. Both fail. Not because you haven't tried hard enough, but because you are trying to fill an infinite space with finite things. This article is about the third option.

You were built for more than you have been given — and you know it.

First, let's be honest about the diagnosis

Here is the claim at the centre of the Christian faith, stated plainly so you can accept or reject it on its merits: you were made by a God who is personal, who is good, and who made you for relationship with Him. That relationship is broken — not because God walked away, but because we did. The Bible has an old, unfashionable word for it: sin. Strip away the caricatures and it simply means this — we insist on being the centre of our own universe, and it is quietly destroying us and the people around us.

You don't need a theologian to prove this. Read the news. Look at your own regrets at 2 a.m. Something is wrong with the world, and — if you are honest — something is wrong in you that you cannot fix by trying harder. Every self-help book you've bought assumes the problem is small enough for you to solve. Deep down you suspect it isn't.

"For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God."Romans 3:23

This is not bad news yet — it is the relief of an accurate diagnosis. You cannot be healed of a disease you refuse to name. And it means your restlessness is not a sign that you are failing at life. It is a sign that you were made for a life you have not yet found.

Now the turn: this is about a person, not a religion

Here is where Christianity refuses to behave like other belief systems. It does not hand you a ladder of rules and say climb. It tells you that God Himself climbed down. Into a specific place — a dusty, occupied corner of the Roman Empire. Into a specific body — hunger, blistered feet, tears at a friend's grave, the smell of sawdust in a carpenter's shop. His name was Jesus of Nazareth, and He made a claim no sane moral teacher makes: that in seeing Him, you were seeing God.

He touched the people everyone else avoided. He told the truth to the powerful and it cost Him. He said the last would be first, that the poor were blessed, that your enemies were to be loved. And then He did the thing that split history in two: He allowed Himself to be executed — publicly, brutally, on a Roman cross — and He said, before He died, that He was doing it for you. Taking the weight of every 2 a.m. regret, every quiet cruelty, every failure, onto Himself, so that the debt you could never repay would simply be paid.

"God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us."Romans 5:8

And on the third day — this is the hinge of the whole story — the tomb was empty. Not a metaphor. Not a nice idea. A claim about a historical event, in a real city, with named witnesses who went to their deaths insisting they had seen Him alive. If it is false, Christianity collapses and you should walk away. If it is true, it changes everything.

"But I have real objections." Good. Let's take them seriously.

You are not naïve, and faith that cannot survive hard questions isn't worth having. Here are the honest questions people your age ask most — and honest answers, not slogans.

01"Hasn't science made God unnecessary?"

Science answers how the universe behaves; it was never built to answer why there is a universe at all, or why it is so precisely tuned that a change of a fraction of a percent in a dozen physical constants would make life impossible. That "fine-tuning" is not preached in churches — it is documented by physicists, believers and atheists alike.

Many of the people who built modern science — Newton, Kepler, Faraday, the Catholic priest Georges Lemaître who first proposed the Big Bang — did so because they believed an orderly Creator meant the universe could be understood. Faith and reason were never enemies. The real question isn't "science or God"; it's "why is there something rather than nothing, and why is it comprehensible at all?"

02"If God is good, why is there so much suffering?"

This is the hardest question, and it deserves more than a paragraph — so we won't insult you with a tidy fix. But notice: the very fact that suffering feels wrong, like a violation of how things ought to be, assumes there is a way things ought to be. Where did that standard come from? A purely random universe has no "ought."

Christianity does not answer suffering with an explanation. It answers with a God who entered it. On the cross, God is not distant from your pain — He is inside it, bleeding. That is a claim no other worldview makes, and it is the only one that has ever helped anyone at a graveside.

03"The church has hurt people. Why would I trust it?"

You're right, and we won't defend the indefensible. Institutions built in Jesus' name have wounded people — and Jesus reserved His sharpest words for exactly that kind of religious hypocrisy. Read the Gospels: the person most furious about self-righteous, judgemental religion is Jesus Himself.

Don't judge a person by the people who misuse His name. Judge Him by Himself. The invitation is to Jesus — not to a perfect institution, because there isn't one, here or anywhere.

04"How can one religion claim to be the only way? Isn't that arrogant?"

It would be arrogant if it were our idea — a way we invented and are demanding you adopt. But the claim isn't that our group is superior; it's that a rescue happened, once, in history, and rescue is by nature specific. If you're drowning and one hand reaches into the water, "there are many hands" is not comforting — the question is whether this one is real. Jesus said, "I am the way." Either He was telling the truth or He wasn't. That's not arrogance; it's a claim you're free to test.

05"Can I trust the Bible? Wasn't it written centuries later and changed?"

This is a factual question with a factual answer. The New Testament is the best-attested document of the ancient world — thousands of early manuscripts, some within a generation of the events, far more and far earlier than anything we have for Caesar or Socrates, whom no one doubts existed. The core accounts trace to eyewitnesses. You may still reject its message — but "it was made up centuries later" is simply not what the historical evidence shows.

06"Won't becoming a Christian mean giving up who I am and just following rules?"

The opposite. Jesus doesn't erase you — He restores the person you were meant to be before the world, and your own fear, pressed you into a smaller shape. The goal isn't rule-keeping; it's freedom. Not "behave, then belong," but belong, and let love change you from the inside out. That is why we speak of transformation, not mere behaviour.

Your generation was promised that endless choice, connection, and self-optimisation would make you free. Instead, anxiety, loneliness, and burnout are at record highs among people born between 1980 and 2000. Perhaps the problem was never that you needed more options. Perhaps you needed an anchor.

So what would it actually change?

Not that your problems vanish — Jesus never promised that. What changes is that you are no longer carrying the universe on your shoulders. You are known completely and loved anyway. Your worth stops depending on your productivity, your follower count, your last performance review. You get a family — real people, at a real address, who will sit with you at 1:47 a.m. And you get a purpose bigger than your own comfort: to love God and love people, and to make a difference in the community and the marketplace where you already stand.

"Come to me, all who labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest."Matthew 11:28

That word — rest — is the one your body has been quietly begging for. Not a holiday. Rest at the level of the soul.

Your next step

You don't have to figure this out alone.

You can respond right now, in your own words — no special language required. If you mean it, pray something like this:

"Jesus, I've been carrying this alone and I'm tired. I believe You are real, that You died and rose for me. I'm done being at the centre. Come in. Make me who I was meant to be."

If you prayed that — or you just have questions and want to talk to a real person — we would love to meet you. Come this Sunday, or reach out first. There is no pressure and no cost.
